End-to-End Battery Optimisation

Following a decent product and demand flexibility through to what it means for settlement risk.

Over the last three years I've been speaking to some very intelligent and passionate people about home battery technology: what it could do for consumer prices, and what it could do for grid optimisation. Almost all of that thinking looks at the front end of the business: sales, product performance, margin, market benefit. Fair enough; that's where the case gets made. But I've been responsible for trade desks for over a decade, and that leaves you with a habit you don't shake: follow every product through to settlement.

So this note follows the thinking through. Not whether home batteries are good products (I think they are, and the first section is the case for them) but whether, at scale, they move enough volume around the day to matter to a supplier's imbalance position. Decentralised solar has already changed the shape of national demand, and EVs are changing it again. The 2021–22 crisis folded twenty-nine suppliers when prices moved faster than balance sheets,1 and I'd like the industry to be able to say, with data rather than optimism, that home batteries aren't building the conditions for a second one: big balancing swings stacked on top of everything else settlement is already absorbing. One thing to be clear about up front: I'm not a battery expert, and I won't pretend to be one here. The people I've been talking to are. My end of the market is what happens when metered volume turns up in a different half-hour from the one you bought.

1. The product

The front-loaded case: buy the cheap hours, sell the dear ones, and the sums work.

The case the product side makes is genuinely strong, and it starts with the shape solar leaves behind. On a clear summer day a solar fleet produces most of its output in the middle of the day, exactly when demand is lowest, so the surplus clears on the wholesale market at the worst possible time: over the second quarter of 2025, after the sunniest spring on record, daytime power prices in Britain fell below overnight prices for the first time ever,2 and at the extreme the price turns negative, so a generator pays to export.3

A battery sits between that surplus and the evening peak: it charges through the cheap midday and overnight hours, holds the energy, and discharges it back into the dear evening. A modern pack returns roughly 85 to 92% of what it takes in, so the round-trip loss is close to the only cost of the trade. The battery earns the gap between the price it charged at and the price it later displaced.

And the savings are real money. The day-ahead peak-to-trough spread a two-hour GB battery could capture ran from under £40/MWh in February 2024 to about £70 that August,4 and stacked across day-ahead arbitrage, imbalance avoidance and the Balancing Mechanism, GB batteries benchmarked near £50k per MW per year in 2024 and about £73k over the twelve months to April 2026.5 None of that is in dispute, and it isn't my subject here. The front of the business has done its sums, and the sums work.

2. So what's the risk to settlement?

Batteries move metered volume through the day, and settlement is where mistimed volume gets priced.

A supplier buys most of its power a day ahead against a forecast of what its customers will use, then every half-hour settles the difference between what it bought and what its meters actually drew, at Elexon's cash-out price, the imbalance price the market balances to, published half-hour by half-hour on Elexon's open API.6 When the forecast is right, the residual is noise. When the forecast is systematically wrong, when the shape of the book has changed underneath the model, the residual is a position, and it settles at some of the most volatile prices in the market. Across the fifteen July 2026 settlement days I pulled for this note, the average cash-out price by half-hour ran from £78/MWh in the middle of the day, the solar trough, to £158 at eight in the evening.6

So the question that matters to the back office is: how far can a home battery actually move a household's metered volume? The observed evidence is early, but it isn't nothing. In Australia (years ahead of Britain on rooftop solar, and now on home batteries) data from 20,000 homes shows battery households drawing an average of about 0.3 kW from the grid across the 4–9pm evening window, against about 1.0 kW for otherwise-similar solar-only homes: roughly 70% of the evening draw moved to other hours of the day.7 At system level, AEMO reported daytime charging across its battery fleet up 166% year on year in Q3 2025, and evening discharge up 177%.8 Britain is behind that curve but accelerating: MCS certified more than 40,000 home battery installations in 2025, almost double the previous record, and battery storage went into 2026 as the fastest-growing segment of the domestic renewables market.9

Britain doesn't yet have an equivalent public dataset, so I built a simple stochastic model to see what the shift could look like here. And I want to be explicit that what follows are my modelled numbers, not observed ones. I simulated fleets of 400 households, 300 Monte Carlo draws per scenario: daily consumption drawn around the typical domestic 2,700 kWh a year,10 solar between 2.5 and 5 kWp with cloud variation, usable battery capacity between 5 and 13.5 kWh, an 85% round trip, and a battery that simply serves its own household (charge from surplus solar, discharge when the house needs more than the roof is making), plus a winter case where a smart tariff charges it from the grid overnight instead.11

In summer, the answer isn't an hour or two, it's the whole day. The plain home imports with the familiar domestic double peak, and a solar-only home still puts about 35% of its (much smaller) import through the 4–9pm window. Add the battery and the modelled evening share collapses to about 4%, with the median half-hour of import moving from 18:30 into the small hours. Winter splits into two very different stories, and this is where I'd push back on my own summer chart. If the battery only does self-consumption, winter barely moves anything: there isn't enough solar to store, so the modelled evening share edges from 43% to 42% and the median import half-hour drifts by about thirty minutes, the "one to two hours at best" end of the range. But put the same battery on a cheap overnight tariff and the picture inverts: total grid import actually rises about 15%, round-trip losses plus charging from the grid, while relocating almost entirely into a synchronised overnight block. The modelled fleet draws a peak of about 3.5 kW per home at 3am, roughly sixteen times the natural overnight draw, and the evening peak is gone.

Priced at settlement, the per-home numbers start small. If a supplier kept forecasting a battery home on a solar-only shape, my summer model puts about 1.8 kWh per home per day in the wrong half-hours: roughly 26p a day per home at July 2026 average cash-out prices, or about 89 MWh a day of misplaced volume across a 50,000-home book.11 I don't think the pennies are the point. The error is one-directional, tariff-synchronised and weather-correlated: whole cohorts of homes move together, overnight blocks switch on and off with tariff changes, and a forecasting model still trained on last year's book shape is wrong the same way in every half-hour at once. That is how small per-meter errors become balancing swings.

3. Early days: but watch the shape

I expect a third category of demand profile to emerge, and forecasting teams to need rebuilding around it.

It's early days, and this note is an opinion backed by a toy model, not a settled result. But I expect the data to show a third category of domestic demand profile emerging: a battery home that is flat against the floor all summer and a synchronised overnight mountain all winter. The industry grew up profiling the classic double-peak home and, lately, the solar household that vanishes at noon and comes back for dinner. Neither looks anything like this.

The timing matters because settlement itself is mid-rebuild. Market-wide Half-Hourly Settlement has been migrating meters since October 2025 (more than two million by February 2026, with around 80% expected by October 2026 and full cutover in 202712), so every one of these battery homes will shortly be settled on its own metered half-hours rather than an assumed profile. That cuts both ways: the real half-hourly shapes finally become visible in settlement, and every assumption built on the old profiles stops working at the same time.

Which is why my conclusion isn't really about batteries at all. It's that supplier forecasting teams need building from the ground up around the data: segment the book by what's bolted to the house, not by what it did last year; detect shape-shift quickly; and treat every new device class as a settlement question from day one, not after the first bad quarter. On the front end, batteries look like the new solar panel: the sums are good. From the back office they look like something bigger. Solar changed how much of a household the grid sees. Batteries change when it sees them at all. I'd rather we followed that through to settlement now, while it's still cheap to be wrong.
